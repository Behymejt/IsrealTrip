A devotional field guide

Galilee to Jerusalem

Thirty-odd stops across ten days, each with the passage that belongs to it, what you are actually standing in, and one question to carry out of the place.

Netanya → Tiberias → Jerusalem Scripture refs in ESV Pastor Brent Snook · Signature Tours

How to use this

Each stop gives you five things: where you are, why it matters in a sentence or two, what to read, what you're looking at, and a question in red. If you only have thirty seconds before the group moves, read the gold “why it matters” line. The red question is the only part that isn't information. Sit with it on the bus.

Passage references are listed rather than printed out — read them from your own Bible, where you can mark them. The one marked key is the passage to read if you only read one.

The seven-week reading plan comes first, then the route map and the history background. A map of the lake sits in Day Four, the elevation profile in Day Seven, and the Old City map in Day Eight, so each one is on the day you need it.

Two labels appear beside site names. Excavated means the stones in front of you are the stones — this is the place, archaeologically. Traditional means the location is commemorative: early, venerated, sometimes very early, but held by memory rather than evidence. Neither label is a verdict on the site's worth. A place people have prayed in for fifteen centuries is doing something a survey marker can't.

Seven weeks of reading

Fifteen minutes a day from 21 September until the night before you fly. It runs in the order of your route rather than the order of the Bible: the coast first, then the lake, then the desert, then Jerusalem. Mark runs the whole way through, a chapter every few days, because Mark travels the same path your bus does, Galilee to the north to Jericho to Jerusalem.

Read it out of your own Bible. The blue tag on each day shows which day of the trip that reading belongs to. If you fall behind, skip to today rather than trying to catch up.

The land, at scale

The first thing most pilgrims say is how small it is. Everything you will visit fits on this one map, and the whole Galilean ministry happened in the little oval at the top.

How far apart things are

Nazareth to Capernaum, where he grew up to where he lived20 mi
Around the Sea of Galilee, end to end13 × 8 mi
Jerusalem to Bethlehem5 mi
Jerusalem to Jericho, and 3,400 ft down14 mi
Capernaum to Jerusalem, the pilgrim road79 mi
Caesarea Philippi to Masada, the whole trip top to bottom135 mi
The Old City of Jerusalem, wall to wall½ mi

All straight-line. Nazareth to Capernaum is about the distance from Amelia to downtown Cincinnati. The whole trip, north to south, is a little longer than Cincinnati to Columbus. A pilgrim family walking from Galilee up to Jerusalem for Passover, as Jesus's did, spent several days on the road.

The layers

Almost every site is a stack. You will hear guides jump between four thousand years in a single sentence, and the eras below are the ones they will be jumping between. The blue line under each era lists the stops on your trip where that layer is visible.

  • c. 9000 BC

    Neolithic

    The first towns anywhere. A stone tower at Jericho is older than the pyramids by about five thousand years.

    Jericho

  • 3000–1200 BC

    Canaanite (Bronze Age)

    Walled city-states, and Egyptian armies marching through the Megiddo pass. Abraham belongs to this world.

    Megiddo · Jericho · the tell at Beit She’an

  • 1000–586 BC

    Israelite kingdoms (Iron Age)

    Saul, David and Solomon, then the divided kingdoms and the prophets. It ends when Babylon burns Solomon’s Temple.

    City of David · Hezekiah’s Tunnel · Elah · Carmel · Megiddo’s gates · En Gedi

  • 586–332 BC

    Exile and return (Persian)

    The exiles come back and build a modest Second Temple in 516 BC. Ezra and Nehemiah.

    Little visible, but it is the platform everything later stands on

  • 332–63 BC

    Greek and Hasmonean

    Alexander, then his successors. The Maccabean revolt that Hanukkah remembers, a brief Jewish kingdom, and the Qumran community settling in the desert.

    Qumran · Beit She’an as Greek Scythopolis · Banias as a shrine to Pan

  • 63 BC–AD 324

    Herod and Rome

    The New Testament world. Herod rebuilds everything, Jesus’s ministry follows, Rome destroys the Temple in AD 70 and takes Masada in 73. After a second revolt in 135, Hadrian renames Jerusalem and bans Jews from it.

    Caesarea · Masada · Western Wall · Magdala · Capernaum’s basalt · Caesarea Philippi · Pool of Siloam · the Gallicantu steps

  • 324–638

    Byzantine Christian

    Constantine’s mother Helena comes in 326 to find the holy sites. Most of the churches you visit were founded in this era, and many of the traditional locations were fixed now.

    Church of the Nativity · the first Holy Sepulchre · Tabgha’s mosaic · Capernaum’s white synagogue · Kursi

  • 638–1099

    Early Islamic

    Caliph Umar takes Jerusalem, and the Dome of the Rock is finished in 691. An earthquake in 749 levels much of Galilee and the Jordan Valley.

    Dome of the Rock · Al-Aqsa · Beit She’an’s fallen columns

  • 1099–1291

    Crusader

    A Latin kingdom for less than two centuries. Saladin retakes Jerusalem in 1187. Much of what you walk through in the Holy Sepulchre is Crusader work.

    Holy Sepulchre · the Upper Room hall · Caesarea’s walls

  • 1260–1917

    Mamluk and Ottoman

    Four centuries of Ottoman rule. Suleiman the Magnificent builds the Old City walls you see today, between 1537 and 1541.

    Every gate and wall of the Old City

  • 1917–today

    British Mandate and modern Israel

    Allenby takes Jerusalem in 1917 and wins at Megiddo in 1918. The Dead Sea Scrolls surface in 1947, the year before the state is founded. Israel takes the Old City in 1967.

    Qumran’s caves · Magdala, found in 2009 · the City of David, still being dug

Day One Monday, 9 November

Leaving

Cincinnati to Newark to Munich to Tel Aviv. Roughly twenty hours of nothing but transit — which is, historically speaking, the shortest pilgrimage to the Holy Land anyone has ever made.

The departure

CVG → EWR → MUC → TLV

  • Psalm 121
  • Psalm 122
  • Genesis 12:1–4

Psalms 120 through 134 are titled Songs of Ascents. They were sung by pilgrims walking up to Jerusalem for the festivals — actual travel music, for people doing on foot what you are doing at 500 knots. Psalm 121 is the one about the journey itself: I lift up my eyes to the hills. It is a song about being kept while you are in motion and away from home.

Read it before you board. Read it again somewhere over the Atlantic. It is the oldest thing anyone has ever said on this trip.

Day Two Tuesday, 10 November

Landfall

Arrival, a transfer up the coast, and a night in Netanya on the Mediterranean. Nothing is asked of you today except to sleep.

The Sharon Plain

Mediterranean coast · Netanya orientation

Why it mattersThe coast where Jonah fled from God and where Peter, centuries later, was sent toward the Gentiles. It was also the main road between Egypt and the empires to the north, so almost every army in the Bible came this way.

  • Isaiah 35:1–2
  • Acts 9:32–43
  • Jonah 1:1–3

The coastal strip you drive up tonight is the Sharon — the plain Isaiah names alongside Lebanon and Carmel when he wants to say glory. South of you is Joppa, where Jonah bought his ticket in the wrong direction and where Peter, centuries later, stayed in a tanner's house and had the rooftop vision that cracked the early church open to the whole world. Two men at the same port, one running from a Gentile city and one being sent to one.

You will land jet-lagged into a modern Israeli resort town with high-rises and a beach promenade. That dissonance is worth noticing rather than resisting. This is a living country, not an exhibit, and the next nine days will keep insisting on it.

Carry thisJonah and Peter stood at the same harbor. What direction am I actually facing?

Practical

Sunset in Israel in mid-November is about 4:40pm — much earlier than you expect. Days are short, which is why mornings start early on this tour.

Get outside into daylight as soon as you can today. It is the single most effective thing for the seven-hour time shift.

Day Three Wednesday, 11 November

The Coast and the Valley

Empire in the morning, prophets at midday, and the hometown that tried to throw him off a cliff. You end the day on the Sea of Galilee.

Caesarea Maritima

Herod's harbor city · Roman provincial capital excavated

Why it mattersThis is where the gospel first went to a non-Jew, when Peter baptized Cornelius. It was Rome’s seat of government in Judea, where Pilate lived, where Paul was tried, and where he sailed from to Rome.

  • Acts 10
  • Acts 21:8–14
  • Acts 25:1–12
  • Acts 26

Herod the Great built this out of nothing in twelve years — no natural harbor, so he sank hydraulic concrete into the sea to make one, and named the whole thing after Augustus. It became the Roman capital of Judea. Pilate lived here; the one stone inscription bearing his name that has ever been found came out of the theater floor in 1961.

But the reason this stop matters devotionally is Cornelius. A Roman centurion in this town sent for Peter, and Peter came, and what happened in that house is the hinge on which the entire Gentile church turns. God has shown me that I should not call any person common or unclean. Paul was later held prisoner here for two years, tried here, and sailed from this harbor to Rome.

You are standing in the room where Christianity stopped being a Jewish sect. Every non-Jewish believer for two thousand years — including you — traces back through a decision made in this town.

Carry thisWhat boundary am I still treating as God's, when it turns out to be only mine?

Mount Carmel

Muhraka · overlooking the Jezreel Valley traditional

Why it mattersThe site of Elijah’s showdown with the prophets of Baal. It is the Old Testament’s great test of whether Israel would worship God alone.

  • 1 Kings 18:20–46
  • 1 Kings 19:1–18

Elijah against four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal, and the question he opened with: How long will you go limping between two different opinions? Not choose God — that comes later. First: stop limping. Stop keeping both options warm.

Stay for the end of the chapter, which is the part people skip. After the fire falls, Elijah climbs to the top and puts his face between his knees and sends his servant to look at the sea seven times, and the seventh time there is a cloud the size of a man's hand. He had just called down fire in front of a nation, and he still had to pray seven times for rain.

The view from here over the Jezreel is one of the great views in Israel. You can see the whole of tomorrow's history at once.

Carry thisWhere am I limping between two opinions — and what would it actually cost to stop?

Megiddo and the Valley of Armageddon

Tel Megiddo · the Jezreel Valley excavated

Why it mattersThe most fought-over pass in the ancient world and the source of the word Armageddon. Twenty-six cities are stacked in one mound, covering most of biblical history.

  • Revelation 16:12–16
  • 2 Kings 23:29–30
  • Judges 5:19–21
  • 1 Kings 9:15

Har-Megiddo — the mount of Megiddo — is where Revelation gets the word Armageddon. The tell beneath you holds twenty-six layers of civilization: Canaanite, Solomonic, Assyrian, each city built on the rubble of the last. Deborah and Barak fought here. Josiah was killed here. Thutmose III, Napoleon, and Allenby all wanted this pass, because whoever holds it holds the road between Egypt and Mesopotamia.

Then you look out from the tell and the valley named for the last battle on earth is, right now, some of the most productive farmland in Israel. Green rectangles, irrigation, tractors. Wheat where the armies gather.

Carry thisThe place named for the end is, today, quiet and fruitful. What does that do to how I hold the future?

Mount Precipice

Above Nazareth · overlooking Jezreel and Mount Tabor traditional

Why it mattersNazareth tried to throw Jesus off this cliff after his first sermon. It marks his rejection by his own hometown, and it looks out over the valley and hills he grew up with.

  • Luke 4:16–30
  • Isaiah 61:1–2

He went to synagogue in his hometown, as was his custom, and read from Isaiah — good news to the poor, liberty to the captives, sight to the blind — and then sat down and said Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing. And they loved it, briefly. Then he kept talking, about a widow in Sidon and a Syrian leper, about God going to outsiders, and they took him to the brow of the hill to throw him down.

Look out from the brow. The rounded hill to the east is Mount Tabor, one traditional site of the Transfiguration. Nain, where he raised a widow’s son, sits on the Hill of Moreh just to its right. Below you is the whole Jezreel Valley you drove through this morning. A boy growing up in Nazareth could see most of Israel’s battle history from his hometown.

His first recorded sermon and the first attempt on his life were the same afternoon, in the town he grew up in, in front of people who had known him since he was small.

Carry thisThey turned not when he claimed too much for himself, but when he said God was generous to outsiders. Which of those would turn me?

Tiberias, where you sleep

West shore of the Sea of Galilee · Caesar Hotel, two nights the place itself

Why it mattersThis was the capital of Herod Antipas, who ruled Galilee for Jesus’s whole life. Centuries later it became the city where the standard Hebrew text of the Old Testament was fixed.

  • John 6:1–2, 22–24
  • John 21:1
  • Luke 13:31–33

Herod Antipas built this town around AD 20 as his new capital and named it for the emperor Tiberius. He built it on top of a graveyard, so observant Jews would not live here at first. The Gospels never record Jesus going in, though Tiberias was a few miles from Capernaum. John is the only Gospel that even names it, and he calls the lake the Sea of Tiberias.

Then history turned it around. After Jerusalem fell, Tiberias became one of the great centers of Jewish learning. Much of the Jerusalem Talmud was compiled here. Between roughly the seventh and tenth centuries, the scholars known as the Masoretes worked out the vowel points and accents of the Hebrew Bible here, letter by letter, the work reaching its peak with the Ben Asher family. The standard Hebrew text behind the Old Testament in your ESV was fixed largely in this town, a short walk from your hotel.

Carry thisA town founded as a Roman ruler’s showpiece became the place the Hebrew Bible was preserved. What in my life is being turned to a use I never planned?

Day Four Thursday, 12 November

The Northern Rim

Up to the edge of the map, to the most pagan place in the region, where he asked the only question that matters. Then back down to the water.

Magdala

Western shore of the Sea of Galilee excavated

Why it mattersMary Magdalene’s hometown. It has one of the very few synagogues ever found from Jesus’s lifetime, which makes it one of the likeliest places on earth where he actually taught.

  • Luke 8:1–3
  • John 20:1–18
  • Mark 1:38–39

This is the sleeper of your whole itinerary. In 2009 they broke ground for a guesthouse and hit a first-century synagogue — one of only a handful anywhere that predate the destruction of the Temple in AD 70. A second one was found nearby in 2021. Inside was a carved stone block, now called the Magdala Stone, decorated by someone who had evidently seen the Jerusalem Temple with his own eyes.

Mark says Jesus went throughout all Galilee preaching in their synagogues. There was one synagogue in Magdala. You are standing in it. This is as close to certainty as this kind of thing gets.

And it was Mary's town. Delivered of seven demons, named first in the list of women who bankrolled the ministry out of their own means, present at the cross when most of the men were not, and the first human being to see the risen Christ — sent to tell the apostles before the apostles knew.

Carry thisThe resurrection was entrusted first to a woman no court of the day would have accepted as a witness. What does that say about who God trusts?

The Golan Heights

Volcanic plateau · biblical Bashan landscape

Why it mattersBiblical Bashan, the high plateau above Galilee. Its animals turn up in Psalm 22, the psalm Jesus quoted from the cross, and it has been a contested frontier from Bible times to today.

  • Psalm 22:12–13
  • Deuteronomy 3:1–11
  • Psalm 68:15–16

Bashan was cattle country — the bulls of Bashan in Psalm 22 are shorthand for something large, well-fed and hostile closing in. That psalm opens with My God, my God, why have you forsaken me, which means the animals of this plateau turn up in the words Jesus spoke from the cross.

You will also see, plainly, that this is a modern military frontier: fencing, bunkers, signs you should take literally. The geography that made this a strategic prize three thousand years ago has not changed, and neither has the fact that people are still contesting it.

Carry thisWhat do I do with the fact that the Bible's landscape is still a place people are fighting over?

Caesarea Philippi

Banias · the grotto of Pan, at the foot of Mount Hermon excavated

Why it mattersThe turning point of the Gospels. Peter first confessed Jesus as the Christ here, and Jesus first told them plainly he would die in Jerusalem. From here the story turns south toward the cross.

  • Matthew 16:13–28
  • Mark 8:27–38

If you only pay close attention at one site this week, make it this one — because here the geography is the sermon.

A spring bursts out of a cliff at the base of Hermon, one of the sources of the Jordan. Beside it is a deep cave the ancients believed was a mouth of the underworld, and around it a shrine to Pan, and niches cut into the rock for other gods, and a temple Herod built to the divinity of Augustus. This was the least Jewish, most crowded-with-gods place in the whole region. Devout people did not come here.

He brought them here and asked: Who do you say that I am? And when Peter answered, he said on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it — standing in front of a literal cave people literally called the gate of hell, surrounded by the niches of dead gods.

He also, in the same conversation, told them plainly for the first time that he was going to Jerusalem to be killed. The confession and the cross arrive together. Peter tried to separate them and got the sharpest rebuke in the Gospels.

Carry thisHe asked it here, not in the Temple. What does it mean that the question is best answered standing among other people's gods?

Kursi

Eastern shore · the Decapolis side traditional

Why it mattersWhere Jesus freed a man possessed by demons, in Gentile country across the lake. The healed man was sent home to tell his own people, which made him one of the first witnesses to non-Jews.

  • Mark 5:1–20
  • Luke 8:26–39

Gentile territory — which is why there were pigs. The man living among the tombs, uncontainable, cutting himself with stones; and then sitting, clothed, in his right mind. The townspeople's reaction is the strange part: they saw him healed and asked Jesus to leave. A restored man was more disruptive than a possessed one.

Then the detail that makes this site worth the stop. The man begged to come along, and Jesus said no — Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you. He is refused the thing he wants most and given a harder assignment: stay where everyone knew what you were.

Carry thisWhat has God asked me to do where I already am, rather than somewhere else?

Yardenit

Where the Jordan leaves the Sea of Galilee modern site

Why it mattersA place to remember Jesus’s baptism in the Jordan, where the Father called him beloved and his public ministry began. It is the same river Israel crossed into the promised land.

  • Matthew 3:13–17
  • Romans 6:1–11
  • Joshua 3:14–17

Be clear-eyed: this is a purpose-built facility, not the historical spot. John baptized far to the south, near Jericho, at what is now Qasr al-Yahud. Yardenit is where the buses can safely stop.

It doesn't diminish what happens here, because what happens here isn't archaeology. It is you standing in the Jordan remembering your own baptism. And the thing to sit with is that at his baptism, before a single miracle, before one sermon, before anything that could be called a track record, the voice said This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased.

The approval came first. The work came after.

Carry thisCan I hear "beloved" before I have produced anything — or do I still need the résumé first?

Day Five Friday, 13 November

The Lake, and the Road South

The densest day of the trip: the water, his home town, the hillside, a Roman city — and then the long climb up to Jerusalem, arriving as Shabbat begins.

On the water

Sea of Galilee · 700 feet below sea level the place itself

Why it mattersJesus calmed the storm, walked on this water, and called fishermen from their boats here. Most of his public ministry happened on or around this lake.

  • Mark 4:35–41
  • Matthew 14:22–33
  • Luke 5:1–11

Thirteen miles by eight, sunk in a basin with the Golan rising to the east, which is exactly why the squalls come down so fast and so hard. Professional fishermen were terrified on this water, and they were terrified for good reason.

Most groups cut the engine partway out and sit. Take the silence rather than the photographs. Why are you so afraid? Have you still no faith? is a question he asked on this specific body of water, and this is the only place on earth you will ever be asked it in situ.

Carry thisWhat storm am I currently managing entirely on my own competence?

Capernaum

His own city · north shore excavated

Why it mattersJesus’s home base during his ministry, and the town with more recorded miracles than any other. Peter lived here, and the synagogue stands on foundations from Jesus’s day.

  • Mark 1:21–34
  • Mark 2:1–12
  • Luke 7:1–10
  • John 6:22–59
  • Matthew 11:20–24

Matthew calls it his own city. He moved here from Nazareth and ran the Galilean ministry out of it. The white limestone synagogue you see is fourth or fifth century, but it sits directly on black basalt foundations from the first — the synagogue the centurion built for them, where he taught the Bread of Life discourse, where the man with the unclean spirit cried out.

A few steps away, under a modern church on pillars, is a first-century house that became a gathering place for Christians extraordinarily early, with prayers scratched into its plaster. The tradition that it was Peter's house is old and serious. This is where the roof came off and the paralytic came down.

And then the hard part. More happened here than anywhere else, and Jesus pronounced judgment on this town specifically: And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? You will be brought down to Hades. It is ruins. Familiarity turned out to be the most dangerous condition of all.

Carry thisWhat have I seen so often that I have stopped being astonished by it?

Tabgha

Loaves and fishes · the Primacy of Peter, on the shore traditional

Why it mattersThe traditional site of the feeding of the five thousand, and of the lakeside breakfast where the risen Jesus restored Peter after his three denials.

  • John 21:1–19
  • Mark 6:30–44

Two churches, a few hundred yards apart. In the first, a fifth-century mosaic of a basket of loaves between two fish, worn smooth by sixteen centuries of feet. In the second, a flat rock at the waterline and the quietest scene in the Gospels.

Peter had denied him three times beside a charcoal fire. Here, after the resurrection, there is another charcoal fire — John uses the same rare word, and he uses it on purpose — and breakfast, and three questions. Simon, son of John, do you love me? Three denials, three askings, three commissions. Jesus does not mention the betrayal. He just rebuilds the man, one question at a time, over breakfast.

This is a good place to stop walking for a few minutes.

Carry thisWhere do I need restoration more than I need forgiveness — and would I recognize the difference?

The Mount of Beatitudes

Hillside above Tabgha, looking down the lake traditional

Why it mattersThis hill remembers the Sermon on the Mount, the longest block of Jesus’s teaching in the Gospels and the heart of what he taught about how to live.

  • Matthew 5:1–16
  • Matthew 5–7 entire
  • Luke 6:17–26

The exact hillside isn't certain; the acoustics of this slope down to the water are famous, and a voice carries remarkably far. Read the Beatitudes here, out loud if you can manage it without self-consciousness.

What tends to land, sitting on this grass, is how small and green and ordinary it is. No amphitheater, no platform. A hill with wildflowers above a lake, and the most consequential nine sentences ever spoken about how to be a human being.

Carry thisWhich beatitude do I least want to be true?

Beit She'an

Scythopolis · Decapolis city, Jordan Valley excavated

Why it mattersThe Philistines hung King Saul’s body on its walls, ending Israel’s first monarchy. Later it was a grand Roman city, and it shows how close the Greek and Roman world was to Jesus’s Galilee.

  • 1 Samuel 31
  • 2 Samuel 1:17–27

People come here for the Bible and leave talking about the Romans. This is one of the best-preserved Roman and Byzantine cities in the country — a colonnaded main street, a theater that still seats a few thousand, bathhouses, public latrines, the whole apparatus of Greco-Roman urban life, twenty miles from Nazareth. It is the correction to any assumption that Jesus grew up in cultural isolation.

Look at the columns lying in rows along the street. They are not a reconstruction. They fell that way in the earthquake of January 749, all in the same direction, and no one ever set them back up.

Underneath all of it is the tell where the Philistines hung the bodies of Saul and his sons on the wall. And then the men of Jabesh-Gilead — whom Saul had rescued at the very beginning of his reign, forty years earlier — marched all night, took the bodies down, and carried them home to bury. They did that for a king who had ended badly, who had lost God's favor, who could do nothing for them. They remembered anyway.

Carry thisWho has ended badly, that I still owe the loyalty of remembering well?

Going up to Jerusalem

Jordan Valley → the Judean hills the ascent

Why it mattersThis is the pilgrim road of the Songs of Ascents. Jesus climbed it for the festivals all his life, and for the last time in the week before he died.

  • Psalm 122
  • Psalms 120–134
  • Luke 2:41–42

In Hebrew you never travel to Jerusalem. You go up — and on this road it is literal, from below sea level in the valley to twenty-five hundred feet in the hills. Every pilgrim in the Bible made this climb, several times a year, on foot, singing.

Read Psalm 122 on the bus as the hills start. I was glad when they said to me, Let us go to the house of the LORD. You have been travelling for five days to say that sentence in the right place.

Carry thisWhat am I actually going up to Jerusalem hoping to find?

Shabbat begins tonight

You arrive Friday, and Shabbat begins at sundown, about 4:40pm. Jerusalem lights candles forty minutes before sunset rather than the usual eighteen, so the city starts shutting down around 4pm. Jerusalem changes character completely: traffic thins, shops shutter, sirens sound at candle-lighting, and much of the city goes quiet until Saturday night.

If the schedule allows it, ask your guide about getting to the Western Wall this evening. Kabbalat Shabbat at the Kotel — crowds singing, circles of students dancing, families arriving — is one of the most extraordinary hours available to a visitor in this city, and it will not repeat during your stay. Your hotel is in East Jerusalem, so take a taxi rather than walking after dark.

Note also that Israeli hotel elevators often run in Shabbat mode from Friday evening, stopping automatically at every floor. This is not a malfunction.

Day Six Saturday, 14 November

Olives and Bethlehem

Shabbat in Jerusalem, so the day runs to Christian and Palestinian sites: the hill he wept from, the garden he sweated in, and the town he was born in.

The Mount of Olives

East of the Old City, across the Kidron the place itself

Why it mattersJesus wept over Jerusalem here, taught about the end of the age here, and ascended from here. The prophet Zechariah says the Lord will stand on this mountain at the end.

  • Luke 19:37–44
  • Acts 1:6–12
  • Matthew 24:1–14
  • Zechariah 14:3–5

The classic view: the whole Temple Mount laid out across the valley, the Dome of the Rock gold in the morning light, the Old City walls behind it. Below you, a Jewish cemetery of roughly 150,000 graves, laid out facing the Temple Mount, because tradition holds the resurrection begins here.

He came over this ridge on a donkey while people shouted, and Luke records that at the exact summit of the acclaim — as he came near and saw the city — he wept over it. Not later, in private. Right then, at the top of the best day of his public life, looking at exactly the view you are looking at.

He also ascended from here, and Zechariah says his feet will stand on this mountain. A great deal of Christian hope is anchored to this specific hillside.

Carry thisWhat does he see when he looks at the thing I am currently celebrating?

The Garden of Gethsemane

Foot of the Mount of Olives · Kidron valley floor excavated

Why it mattersWhere Jesus prayed “not my will, but yours” and was arrested. It is the place he accepted the cross.

  • Luke 22:39–46
  • Matthew 26:36–56
  • Hebrews 5:7–9

Gethsemane means oil press — a garden with equipment for crushing olives, which is not a symbolism anyone had to invent afterward. Some of the trees here have root systems many centuries old; olives regenerate from the root, so these may well descend from trees that stood here that night.

Understand what actually happened in this garden. The cross was not decided at the trial or on Golgotha. It was decided here, in the dark, sweating, alone, with his closest friends asleep a few yards away, asking three times for another way and being told no. Not my will, but yours, be done. Everything after this is execution of a decision already made on this ground.

The church built over the rock is deliberately kept dim, with violet windows. Sit in it for a while rather than photographing it.

Carry thisWhat am I asking God to remove, that he may instead be asking me to carry?

Bethlehem · Church of the Nativity

West Bank · six miles south of Jerusalem traditional, very early

Why it mattersThe traditional birthplace of Jesus, honored as such since the second century. It is the oldest church in the world still in continuous use.

  • Luke 2:1–20
  • Micah 5:2–5
  • Matthew 2:1–18

The oldest continuously operating church in the world. Justinian's basilica from the sixth century, standing on Constantine's from the fourth, over a grotto that Christians were already identifying as the birthplace by around AD 150 — within living memory of people who would have known.

You enter through the Door of Humility, an opening barely four feet high, cut down over the centuries partly to stop looters riding horses inside. Whatever the original reason, the effect is exact: nobody enters this church standing upright. Everyone stoops.

Note where you are, too. Bethlehem is a Palestinian city; you cross a checkpoint to reach it. Its historically Christian population has fallen sharply over the last century. The people selling you olive-wood carvings are, many of them, from families who have been Christians here since before England was.

Carry thisWhat am I going to have to stoop to get through?

Shepherds' Fields

Beit Sahour, east of Bethlehem traditional

Why it mattersThe angels announced the birth to shepherds here. These fields around Bethlehem are also where Ruth gleaned and where David kept his father’s sheep.

  • Luke 2:8–20
  • Ruth 2
  • 1 Samuel 16:1–13

These same fields are where Ruth gleaned behind Boaz's harvesters — David's great-grandmother, a Moabite widow, working for scraps in the town her descendant would be born in. And where David was keeping the sheep when Samuel arrived looking for a king and nobody thought to call him in from the field.

Then the announcement. Shepherds worked the night shift, lived outdoors with animals, were ritually unclean much of the time, and were considered unreliable witnesses in legal proceedings. That is who got told. God's announcement of the incarnation went to the one category of people whose testimony a court would have thrown out.

Carry thisWho would I not have thought to tell?

The Valley of Elah

Shephelah · between Socoh and Azekah the place itself

Why it mattersWhere David killed Goliath. It is one of the easiest Bible battlefields to picture, because the landscape has barely changed.

  • 1 Samuel 17

One of the most legible battlefields in the Bible. Philistines on one ridge, Israelites on the other, the valley floor between them, and the brook running through the middle. You can stand where the armies faced off and see the entire tactical situation at a glance — and you can pick five smooth stones out of that brook, which is exactly what a great many pilgrims do.

The detail worth carrying is not the sling. It is that Saul dressed David in his own armor first, and David walked a few steps and took it off, because he had not tested it. He went down to the brook as himself.

Carry thisWhose armor am I currently trying to fight in?

Day Seven Sunday, 15 November

Down to the Dead Sea

The longest descent on earth, from the hills to the lowest point on the planet. Wilderness, scrolls, a fortress, an oasis, and one of the oldest towns on earth.

The Judean Wilderness

Jerusalem → the Dead Sea · 2,500 ft above to 1,400 ft below the place itself

Why it mattersThe desert of Jesus’s forty days, John the Baptist’s preaching, and David’s years on the run. It is the landscape behind Psalm 23 and the parable of the Good Samaritan.

  • Psalm 23
  • Matthew 4:1–11
  • Luke 10:25–37
  • Hosea 2:14–15

Watch the window on the way down. In under twenty miles you drop nearly four thousand feet and the world turns to bare chalk hills, wadis, and nothing. A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho — down is not a figure of speech, and once you have seen this road, the Samaritan story is a different story.

Read Psalm 23 here rather than anywhere else. Green pastures and still water are not pastoral decoration in this landscape; they are survival, and they are rare, and a shepherd who finds them has done something. The valley of the shadow of death is the wadi you are currently driving through.

This is also where he spent forty days, and where John preached, and where the scapegoat was driven on the Day of Atonement. Scripture keeps sending people out here on purpose.

Carry thisWhat has the wilderness actually been for, in my life — and did I treat it as punishment when it was preparation?

Qumran

Northwest shore of the Dead Sea excavated

Why it mattersThe Dead Sea Scrolls were found in these caves, including the oldest known copies of books of the Hebrew Bible. They showed how faithfully scripture had been copied over a thousand years.

  • Isaiah 40:1–8
  • 2 Timothy 3:14–17

A separatist community lived out here copying scripture for roughly two centuries, and when the Romans came in AD 68 they hid their library in jars in the cliffs. A Bedouin boy found the first cave in 1947.

Among them was a complete scroll of Isaiah, a thousand years older than any Hebrew manuscript previously known — and substantially, unremarkably, the same text. That single result is one of the great quiet moments in the history of biblical scholarship.

Stand and look at the caves. Men you will never know the names of sat in this heat with reed pens, copying a text letter by letter, for readers who would not exist for two thousand years. You own a Bible partly because somebody put one in a jar.

Carry thisWhat am I building or preserving for people I will never meet?

Masada

Herod's fortress · 1,300 ft above the Dead Sea excavated

Why it mattersHerod’s desert fortress and the last stand of the Jewish revolt against Rome in AD 73. It shows where the violent path ends, the one Jesus refused.

  • John 6:14–15
  • Matthew 26:47–56
  • John 18:33–37

Herod built a palace on a plateau in the desert with cisterns, storerooms, a bathhouse, and a villa hanging off the northern cliff in three tiers. About seventy-five years after his death, Jewish rebels held it against Rome, and the Tenth Legion built a siege ramp that is still there, still visible, still the most eloquent object on the site. Josephus says that when the wall was breached in the spring of AD 73, the defenders — 960 of them, by his count — were already dead by their own hand. He is the only source, and archaeologists have never found physical evidence of a mass suicide, so some scholars think the end was messier than he tells it.

No Gospel event happened here. It matters anyway, because it shows you the road not taken. The zealot option was live and attractive in Jesus' lifetime — a crowd tried to make him king by force, Peter drew a sword in the garden, and he refused both times. My kingdom is not of this world. Masada is where that other path ends.

Carry thisWhat does it cost to refuse the sword when the sword is right there and the cause is just?

En Gedi

Oasis and waterfall in the Dead Sea cliffs the place itself

Why it mattersDavid spared King Saul’s life in a cave here, when he could have killed him, and wrote Psalm 57 while hiding. It is a real oasis in the middle of the desert.

  • 1 Samuel 24
  • Psalm 57
  • Song of Songs 1:14

Freshwater falling through a slot in absolute desert, with ibex on the rocks. After hours in the wilderness it is almost shocking, which is the point of every oasis image in scripture.

David hid in these caves while Saul hunted him, and in one of them Saul came in alone and David had him — had the man who had thrown spears at him, had the throne already promised to him, had every argument on his side. And he cut the corner off Saul's robe instead, and then his conscience troubled him even for that.

Psalm 57 is headed “when he fled from Saul, in the cave.” Which cave it means is not recorded, and tradition has attached it both to En Gedi and to Adullam — but there is no better place to read it than this one.

Carry thisWhat could I do to someone right now, with the power and arguably the right — that I should not do?

Jericho and the Mount of Temptation

Tell es-Sultan · among the oldest towns on earth excavated

Why it mattersAmong the oldest inhabited places on earth, and the first city Israel took in the promised land. On his last trip to Jerusalem, Jesus healed blind Bartimaeus and ate with Zacchaeus here.

  • Luke 19:1–10
  • Mark 10:46–52
  • Joshua 6
  • Matthew 4:1–11

Eleven thousand years of settlement, with more than twenty towns built one on top of another. Its Neolithic stone tower, carbon-dated to about 8300 BC, predates the pyramids by well over five thousand years. Joshua's walls, Rahab, Elisha's spring, Herod's winter palace. Above the town, a monastery clings improbably to the cliff face at the traditional site of the temptation.

But the scene to sit with is Zacchaeus. A collaborator, hated, rich on his neighbors' money, too short to see, undignified enough to climb a tree. Jesus stops under it, calls him by name, and invites himself to lunch. The last thing he does before going up to Jerusalem to die is eat in the house of the most despised man in town.

Also here: blind Bartimaeus, shouting over a crowd that told him to be quiet, and the question Jesus asked him — What do you want me to do for you?

Carry thisWho is up the tree — visible, ridiculous, hoping — that I have walked straight past?

The Dead Sea

Lowest point on the surface of the earth the place itself

Why it mattersThe lowest place on earth and the setting of Sodom and Gomorrah. The prophet Ezekiel pictured this lifeless sea made fresh by a river flowing from God’s temple.

  • Ezekiel 47:1–12
  • Genesis 19:23–29

Ten times saltier than the ocean. Nothing lives in it. You will float absurdly, take the obligatory photograph, and discover that the water stings anywhere you have shaved.

Before you get in, read Ezekiel 47: the prophet's vision of a river running east from the Temple, getting deeper as it goes, and where it empties into this sea the water will become fresh. Fish in it. Trees on both banks, fruit every month, leaves for healing. Written by a man in exile about the deadest water on earth.

Floating in it while holding that image is one of the better juxtapositions available to a traveler anywhere.

Carry thisWhat part of me have I written off as too salted to live — and what would it mean for the river to reach it?

Day Eight Monday, 16 November

Inside the Walls

The most contested ground on earth, a retaining wall people have wept at for centuries, a route through a working bazaar, and a church six denominations cannot agree about.

The Temple Mount

Haram esh-Sharif · 35 acres excavated

Why it mattersSolomon’s Temple and Herod’s Temple both stood here, and Jesus taught here during his last week. It is the holiest site in Judaism and one of the holiest in Islam.

  • Mark 11:15–19
  • Mark 12:41–44
  • Mark 13:1–2
  • Genesis 22:1–14
  • John 4:19–26

Solomon's Temple stood here, then the Second Temple, then Herod's vast expansion of the platform you are walking on. Tradition puts Abraham and Isaac on this rock. Since 691 the Dome of the Rock has stood over it, and since 70 AD there has been no Temple.

In his last week Jesus taught here daily. He overturned the tables here. He watched a widow put in two small coins here and said she had given more than everyone. And his disciples said look at these stones and he said not one will be left on another — a statement so implausible, looking at the Herodian ashlars, that it must have sounded insane.

Hold alongside all of it what he told a Samaritan woman at a well: the hour is coming when the question of this mountain or that one stops being the question.

Carry thisWhat am I holding as the necessary place — the building, the format, the tradition — that God may not be holding at all?

Rules on the Mount

Non-Muslim visitors enter only by the Mughrabi Bridge beside the Western Wall, during limited morning windows, Sunday to Thursday. It is closed to visitors on Fridays, Saturdays and Muslim holidays, and it can shut at short notice on any day; your guide will know how things stand. Your Temple Mount morning is a Monday, normally a visiting day.

No religious items, no Bibles, and no visible prayer — this is enforced, and bags are checked. Dress modestly: shoulders and knees covered, for men as well as women. Non-Muslims may not enter the Dome of the Rock or Al-Aqsa themselves.

The Western Wall

The Kotel · Herodian retaining wall excavated

Why it mattersThe surviving retaining wall of Herod’s Temple platform. It is the closest point to where the Temple stood at which Jews can pray today.

  • 1 Kings 8:22–43
  • Psalm 137
  • Lamentations 1:1–5

It is worth being precise about what this is: not the Temple, but a section of the retaining wall Herod built to hold up the platform. It is venerated because it is the closest surviving structure to where the Holy of Holies stood.

Solomon prayed at the dedication that when people turned toward this house from anywhere, God would hear. Two and a half thousand years later, people are still turning toward it — and pushing folded paper into the cracks, which you are entirely welcome to do. Men and women approach separate sections; men cover their heads, and paper kippot are provided.

Whatever your theology of the place, watching people pray with that much conviction at a wall holding up an absence is a lesson in persistence.

Carry thisWhat am I going to write on that piece of paper — and why haven't I said it out loud?

The Via Dolorosa

Muslim Quarter → Christian Quarter · fourteen stations devotional route

Why it mattersThe traditional route of Jesus carrying the cross to his crucifixion. Pilgrims have walked it in prayer for centuries.

  • Luke 23:26–43
  • John 19:16–22
  • Mark 15:16–32

Be straightforward about this one: the route is medieval, fixed by Franciscans centuries later, and several stations commemorate events that are traditional rather than scriptural. The street level of the first century is many feet below where you are walking.

And it is still worth doing, for a reason that surprises most people. You expect solemnity and you get a working bazaar — vendors, handcarts, schoolchildren, shopkeepers on phones, a man selling bread who wants you to move. It is loud and commercial and entirely indifferent.

Which is precisely what it was like. It was an ordinary Friday. Jerusalem was crowded for Passover, business was brisk, and for almost everyone in this city that day, nothing whatsoever happened. The indifference is not a failure of the site. It is the most historically accurate thing about it.

Carry thisWhat was I busy with, the last time something unbearable was happening to someone within a hundred yards of me?

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre

Christian Quarter · Golgotha and the tomb, under one roof excavated

Why it mattersThe most likely place of Jesus’s crucifixion, burial and resurrection, and the holiest site in Christianity.

  • John 19:38–42
  • Matthew 27:57–66
  • Luke 24:1–12

The historical case here is stronger than most Protestants expect. In the first century this spot was outside the city wall; it was a disused quarry with rock-cut tombs in its face; Hadrian built a pagan temple over it in the 130s, which is itself evidence that Christians were venerating the site early enough to be worth suppressing; and Constantine's people demolished that temple and found a tomb. Most archaeologists, including Protestant ones, take it seriously.

What you will experience is chaos. It is dark, thick with incense, loud, and crowded, and it is shared under a fragile arrangement by six denominations who have occasionally come to blows in it. A wooden ladder has sat under a window since the eighteenth century because no one is permitted to move it. A Muslim family has held the key since the twelfth century, because the Christians could not be trusted with it.

Somewhere in that is the most honest picture of the church you will ever be handed: divided, stubborn, ancient, quarrelsome, and still — after two thousand years — refusing to leave the tomb.

Carry thisCan I love a church that looks like this? Because this is what it looks like.

Day Nine Tuesday, 17 November

Zion, and the Garden

The upper room, the oldest stones in Jerusalem, and then a quiet garden with a stone tomb, where your group will take communion and where the trip is designed to end.

Mount Zion

The Cenacle, Gallicantu, and the Dormition traditional

Why it mattersThe traditional site of the Last Supper and of Pentecost, when the church began. Caiaphas’s house, where Peter denied Jesus, is on the same hill.

  • Luke 22:7–34
  • Acts 1:12–14
  • Acts 2:1–42
  • Luke 22:54–62
  • Psalm 88

The Upper Room you will stand in is a Crusader-era hall, not a first-century one — but the tradition that the Last Supper and Pentecost happened on this hill is very old. A rented room upstairs is where the church was instituted and where it was ignited, fifty days apart.

Then walk down to St. Peter in Gallicantu — the cock's crow — on the traditional site of Caiaphas's house. Beneath the church is a pit cut into bedrock, reachable only by rope, where prisoners were held. If Jesus was held overnight at the high priest's house, he was held in something very like it, and possibly in that one.

Psalm 88 is traditionally read in that pit. It is the one psalm of lament that never resolves — no turn, no comfort, ending on the word darkness. Somebody should read it aloud down there.

Outside, an ancient stepped street runs down toward the Kidron. Those stairs are first century. If he walked from the upper room to Gethsemane, he walked on those stones.

Carry thisPeter failed here, at night, in a courtyard, three times — and was still given the church. What do I think my worst night has disqualified me from?

The City of David

The original Jerusalem · the ridge south of the Temple Mount excavated

Why it mattersThe original Jerusalem that David captured and made his capital. It holds Hezekiah’s Tunnel and the Pool of Siloam, where Jesus healed a man born blind.

  • 2 Kings 20:20
  • John 9:1–12
  • 2 Samuel 5:6–10
  • Psalm 46

The first surprise is how small it is. David's Jerusalem was a narrow spur of about eleven acres — you could walk its length in five minutes. Everything in the Old Testament happened in a city smaller than a shopping centre.

Hezekiah's Tunnel is the highlight. In 701 BC, with the Assyrian army coming, two crews cut seventeen hundred feet through solid rock from opposite ends and met in the middle. Water still runs through it. If you walk it, you go knee-deep in the dark for half an hour, in a channel cut by frightened men under deadline, and come out at the Pool of Siloam — where the man born blind was sent to wash.

And the pilgrimage road from that pool up to the Temple has now been excavated. Those are the actual steps festival pilgrims climbed, singing the psalms you read on the bus coming down from Galilee.

Carry thisAll of it started this small. What am I despising in my own life for being too small to matter?

The Garden Tomb

Outside the Damascus Gate · communion service devotional site

Why it mattersA quiet garden with an ancient tomb, where Protestant pilgrims gather to remember the resurrection and take communion together.

  • John 20:1–18
  • Luke 24:1–12
  • 1 Corinthians 11:23–26

Honesty first, because you would rather have it: this site was identified in the 1880s, the escarpment beside it looks like a skull from certain angles, and the tomb itself is almost certainly several centuries too old to be the new tomb in which no one had yet been laid. The archaeological case belongs to the Holy Sepulchre. The Garden Tomb's own guides will generally tell you so.

What it is instead is a quiet green garden with benches, a few hundred yards from the noise, where Protestant groups can do the thing that is impossible in the scrum of the Sepulchre: sit down together, read the resurrection account slowly, and take communion. Your tour saves it for last on purpose. For most groups it is the hour people actually remember.

The line to sit with is the one Jesus says to Mary in the garden, before she recognizes him — Whom are you seeking? It is the same question he asked at the very beginning of John's Gospel, of the first two people who ever followed him. The book closes the loop.

Carry thisWhom am I seeking? Not what — whom. Answer it here, before you fly home.

The farewell dinner

Jerusalem · last night closing

  • Luke 24:13–35

The Emmaus road is the right passage for tonight. Two people walking away from Jerusalem, disappointed, explaining the whole thing badly to a stranger who turns out to be the subject of it. They only recognize him at dinner, at the breaking of bread, and then he is gone and they say to each other: Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road?

Ten days of roads and talking. Ask the table what burned.

Day Ten Wednesday, 18 November

Going Home

Tel Aviv, Frankfurt, Dulles, Cincinnati. The part nobody prepares for.

Re-entry

TLV → FRA → IAD → CVG

  • Acts 1:1–11
  • Luke 24:44–53
  • 2 Corinthians 3:2–3

The disciples' last question was about timetables — is this when you restore the kingdom? — and he refused to answer it, and gave them an assignment instead: you will be my witnesses, here, then nearby, then everywhere. Carry thisWhat is the one thing I am going to do differently — and who am I going to tell, this week, so that it costs me something to drop it?

Each night, four lines

Ten days blur badly, and you will not invent a good structure at 10pm in Tiberias. Same four prompts, every night, five minutes. Write in the same notebook you brought for the trip and nowhere else.

  • Where we were today, in one sentence.
  • The passage that landed, and the line from it.
  • What surprised me — what was not how I had pictured it.
  • The question I am still carrying.

Bring: your own ESV with margins you're willing to write in, a pen that works, that notebook, and broken-in shoes with real tread. The Judean desert day and the Old City day are both hard on feet, and Jerusalem limestone is polished slick by two millennia of pilgrims.

Ask your guide things. Israeli guides complete a demanding two-year licensing course and many are formidable — historians, archaeologists, people with strong and well-informed opinions. Ask them the contested questions, not just the itinerary ones. It is the single highest-yield thing you can do on this trip, and most travelers never do it.
